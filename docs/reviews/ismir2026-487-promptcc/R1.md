## Q5 — Related work justification

The paper cites relevant work on text-to-music generation, pseudo-captioning, quality-aware conditioning, and reward conditioning. However, I do not think it fully discusses all relevant related work. In particular, the method is not only about pseudo-captioning; it can also be viewed as distilling information from a pretrained music understanding/captioning system into a generative model. There is prior work in music/audio generation along these lines, such as systems that use pretrained audio taggers or other teacher models to provide semantic conditioning or soft labels for controllable generation. This line of work should be discussed to better contextualize the proposed prompt-consistency signal. The paper would also benefit from clearer positioning with respect to confidence/uncertainty estimation, pseudo-label reliability, and label-noise-aware training, since the proposed q-value is essentially derived from the stochastic agreement of a pseudo-labeling system.

## Q10 — Scientific quality justification

The proposed idea is creative and relevant, but I do not find the current experimental evidence strong enough to support the main claims. The reported objective improvements are generally small and sometimes mixed, with no variance estimates, multiple training seeds, or statistical significance tests. The subjective study is useful, but it compares only against the direct baseline and is not accompanied by audio examples or a demo page that would help assess the perceptual magnitude of the improvement. More importantly, the proposed “prompt consistency” score measures agreement among generated captions in text-embedding space, not whether those captions are correct or well grounded in the audio. This makes the interpretation of the conditioning signal unclear.

## Q15 — Reusable insights

The paper proposes a potentially useful idea: using the stochastic agreement of an automatic captioning model as an additional conditioning signal for text-to-music training. However, the reusable insight is limited by the current analysis. It is not yet clear whether the q-value captures captioner confidence, semantic ambiguity, caption diversity, audio-caption correctness, or simply a partition of the pseudo-captioned training data. Since the consistency score is computed only in text space, it may not reflect the suitability of a caption for the audio. The method may still be useful as a training regularizer, but the paper does not provide enough evidence to establish a general principle or mechanism.

## Q18 — Take-home message

PromptCC is a simple and potentially useful way to condition text-to-music training on the (semantic) agreement of a generative pseudo-captioner for a given audio track, but the paper does not yet convincingly show what the consistency signal captures or that the reported gains are robust.

## Q23 — Main review and comments for the authors

This paper proposes Prompt-Consistency Conditioning (PromptCC), a method for training text-to-music models from unlabeled audio using automatically generated captions. For each audio clip, the authors generate multiple captions using the same music captioning model with different random seeds, compute the mean pairwise similarity among these captions in a sentence-embedding space, quantize this score, and inject the resulting q-value as an additional conditioning signal into a MeanAudio-based text-to-music model.

The idea is simple, creative, and relevant. Pseudo-captioning is widely used in text-to-music and text-to-audio training, and it is reasonable to ask whether a model can benefit from knowing how stable or unstable the captioning system is for a given audio clip. I also appreciate that the authors compare against several natural alternatives, including no PromptCC, hard filtering, CLAP conditioning, PQ conditioning, and a version without quantization. The subjective study is also a positive aspect of the paper: the authors report a paired CMOS test with confidence intervals, p-values, and Bonferroni correction.

However, I am not fully convinced by the paper in its current form.

My main conceptual concern is that “prompt consistency” is not clearly defined in relation to the audio. The proposed score measures agreement among several generated captions in text-embedding space. This can capture the stability of the captioning model under stochastic decoding, but it does not necessarily measure caption correctness, audio-caption alignment, or label quality. Five generated captions could be mutually similar and still be wrong; conversely, five captions could be semantically diverse and all valid descriptions of the same music. This distinction is important because the paper sometimes frames the q-value as if it directly addresses the “caption consistency issue,” but the actual signal is closer to text-space agreement or semantic dispersion among pseudo-labels. I would encourage the authors to define this more precisely and avoid conflating captioner agreement with audio-grounded correctness.

Relatedly, the interpretation of the q-value remains unclear. At different points, it seems to be described as a consistency signal, a confidence signal, a way of indicating ambiguity, and a way of helping the model decide how much to trust the pseudo-caption. These are related but not identical. The paper would be stronger with a qualitative analysis of low-, mid-, and high-q examples, including the generated captions and the corresponding audio. Since the authors mention that very few samples fall below q < 5, these cases could be manually inspected to understand what the score actually captures.

The empirical evidence is also weaker than I would like. In Table 1, the objective improvements over the baseline are generally small, and the results are mixed on the MTG-Jamendo test set. For example, PromptCC improves some AES scores but underperforms the baseline in CLAP, PE-AV, and PC on MTG-Jamendo. On MusicCaps, the improvements are more consistent, but the paper provides no variance estimates, confidence intervals, significance tests, or multiple training seeds for these objective metrics. This makes it difficult to assess whether the gains are robust. The subjective evaluation is useful, but without accompanying audio examples or a demo page, it is hard for the reader to judge the practical perceptual significance of the improvements.

I also find some of the baselines insufficiently diagnostic. The hard-filtering baseline removes audio clips with low consistency and reduces the training data by 53%. Its poor performance therefore does not necessarily show that filtering inconsistent pseudo-labels is a bad idea; it may simply show that discarding half of the training data hurts. A more informative comparison would control for dataset size, filter or resample captions rather than audio, or regenerate captions until a stronger agreement criterion is met. Similarly, the “PromptCC without quantization” baseline appears to prepend the scalar score to the text prompt. This is a rather weak implementation of continuous conditioning and may not be a fair test of whether quantization itself is necessary.

The use of MTG-Jamendo for evaluation should also be clarified. MusicCaps has human-written captions and is therefore a more convincing evaluation set for prompt following. In contrast, MTG-Jamendo test captions appear to be generated by the same or similar pseudo-captioning process used during training. This risks making the evaluation somewhat endogamous: the model is evaluated using prompts from the same captioning distribution that created the training labels. If MTG-Jamendo is included, the authors should explain what this evaluation is intended to measure and how potential pseudo-caption bias is handled.

Some technical details and terminology also need clarification. The paper describes MeanAudio as a flow-matching model, but MeanAudio appears to involve mean-flow training for one-step generation. The paper should more clearly explain what is inherited from MeanAudio, what is modified, and how the two-stage training with flow matching and mean flow matching is implemented. The description of CFG is also somewhat ambiguous: condition dropout is used during training, while guidance scale is an inference parameter. Since the model uses one-step synthesis after mean-flow fine-tuning, the relationship between CFG, condition dropout, and the second-stage training should be explained carefully.

The writing is generally understandable, but it is repetitive and could be better organized. Some of the introduction and related-work material repeats the same motivation, while important methodological details are introduced quickly or only later in the experiments. A clearer background/method split would help: first explain the pseudo-captioning problem, then MeanAudio/MeanFlow and the conditioning mechanism, then the proposed consistency score and embedding.

Finally, I appreciate the explicit AI usage statement and did not notice obvious hallucinated references. The disclosure is clear and useful.

Overall, I think the idea is interesting and may be practically useful, but the current paper does not provide sufficient evidence for acceptance. The method is a lightweight conditioning trick whose mechanism remains under-analyzed, and the reported improvements are small, mixed, and not supported by enough statistical analysis or perceptual examples. I would encourage the authors to resubmit with stronger evidence: multiple seeds or confidence intervals for objective metrics, a public demo page or audio examples, a clearer analysis of what q captures, stronger baselines for filtering/conditioning, and a more careful distinction between text-space caption agreement and audio-grounded caption correctness.
