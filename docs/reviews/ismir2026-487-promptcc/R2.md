## Q15 — Reusable insights

The paper identifies a real issue in unsupervised TTM training: auto-generated captions for the same music clip can vary, and exposing this variation to the generator may help training. The reusable insight is that caption consistency can be treated as an auxiliary training signal rather than handled only by filtering or caption selection.

However, the insight is limited because the consistency score is derived from repeated sampling of a single captioning model, so it may reflect that model’s internal bias or sampling behavior rather than true caption reliability.

The paper also does not convincingly establish that this signal generalizes beyond one captioner, one TTM backbone, and one training pipeline.

---

## Q18 — Take-home message

The paper proposes conditioning TTM training on the consistency of auto-generated captions, but the contribution appears incremental and its generality is not yet well established.

## Q23 — Main review and comments for the authors

The paper points that when captions are generated automatically, the same music clip may receive multiple plausible but inconsistent text descriptions. The proposed PromptCC method is clear and reasonable: generate multiple captions with the same captioning model, compute their semantic consistency, quantize it, and inject it as an additional conditioning signal during TTM training.

However, my main concern is the size and depth of the contribution. The method is essentially an additional discretized conditioning signal derived from caption consistency, injected through a trainable embedding. This is a natural extension of prior quality-aware or reward-conditioning approaches, and the paper does not fully convince me that the conceptual or technical novelty is large enough on its own. The idea is sensible, but it feels more like a useful training heuristic than a substantial new contribution.

A second concern is that the meaning of the consistency score is not cleanly established. The score is computed from repeated generations of a single captioning model. Low consistency may indicate caption ambiguity, but it may also reflect sampling noise or limitations of the captioner. High consistency may indicate reliable captions, but it may also indicate a stable bias or stable error from the same captioning model. The method therefore does not clearly separate caption consistency from caption correctness, coverage, or bias.

The experimental evidence is also somewhat limited. All main experiments use one TTM backbone, MeanAudio, and one captioning model, LP-MusicCaps. This makes it hard to know whether PromptCC is a generally useful method or a pipeline-specific trick. The objective metrics in Table 1 are mixed and not fully convincing: improvements appear stronger on MusicCaps, while the MTG-Jamendo test set shows mixed behavior across CLAP, PE-AV, and AES components. The subjective CMOS result is more persuasive and suggests that PromptCC may improve perceived quality and prompt following, but the study is relatively small and only compares against the baseline without PromptCC.

Overall, I find the problem relevant and the proposed method reasonable, but I am not convinced that the contribution is strong enough in its current form. The paper would be more convincing with experiments across multiple captioning models and TTM backbones, a clearer analysis of what the consistency score actually captures, and stronger comparisons against alternative multi-caption or caption-quality strategies. My evaluation is therefore negative mainly because the idea appears incremental and the evidence for generality is limited.
